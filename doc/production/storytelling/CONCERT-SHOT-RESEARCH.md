# How concert films and live-music TV use shot size, movement and cutting across a song

From mix-assist, 2026-10-19. This is a literature check for the BOTTB Sydney 2026 auto-cut. Four or
five angles are available: Static Desk (wide), Desk Tracking, Photo Pit (close), Drum, and Stage
Right. It tests Dean's stated practice ("at big moments… wide shots zooming out", refined to "after
big peaks as we feel things recede") against what directors and studies say.
Nothing was played. Every quote below comes from a page or PDF opened in this session.

**Evidence labels**

- **[D] Documented practice:** several independent sources agree.
- **[N] Named director's stated practice:** one person, quoted.
- **[O] Opinion:** a blog, forum post or critic.
- **[S] Study:** empirical work (the sample is noted).

**How each source was read.** Some sources were downloaded and read as full text: Zettl, Cutting,
Cutting & Armstrong, Prétet & Richard, Pohl, Caston, the RTS Dugdale interview and the Southampton
call for papers. Their quotes are exact. The other pages were read through a fetch tool that returns
excerpts. Their quotes are verbatim fragments as returned, and some are shortened where marked "…".

**Could not open:**

- Rolling Stone, Billboard and Hollywood Reporter (paywall redirects);
- British Cinematographer's Dugdale/Turnbull articles, Sound & Vision's Elton John feature and
  Gramophone (403 errors);
- the Interference interview with Hamish Hamilton (403);
- the NAB Amplify write-up of Sam Wrench's session (404);
- Film Comment's 1984 Demme piece (404);
- the IEEE/IRIS Benini paper (403).

Claims that rest only on those sources are not used.

## 2. Shot size by song section (question 1)

**Chorus, peak or climax: tighter and faster. [D]**

- Zettl, _Television Production Handbook_ (3rd ed., 1976), ch. 10 "Picturization, or Editing"
  ([PDF](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/BOOKSHELF-ARH/Education/Television-Production-Handbook-Zettl-3rd-1976.pdf)):

  > "(4) To change impact. A cut to a tighter shot generally intensifies the screen event; a cut to a
  > longer shot reduces the event impact. (5) To establish an event rhythm. … Fast cutting generally
  > gives the impression of excitement; slow cutting that of calm and tranquility"

  On "complexity editing" (intensification), the same chapter says:

  > "Closeup Cutting to a closeup, or even extreme closeup, cannot fail to direct the viewer's
  > attention forcefully to event detail. Thus, the event is perceived more intensely than if shown
  > in a medium or long shot."

- Maxime Pozzi, a music-video editor (Rihanna, Taylor Swift), interviewed in Prétet & Richard, "Is
  there a 'language of music-video clips'?", ISMIR 2021 ([PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.00970)):

  > "For chorus and verses, the editing will follow the rhythm and typically accelerate near climaxes.
  > During bridges, it will often be slower and poetic."

  Alexandre Courtès (director for U2 and Franz Ferdinand), in the same paper:

  > "At large temporal scale, their mission is to emphasize the music climaxes by the appropriate
  > video content."

- Pohl, _Editing Shaped the Video Star_ (BA thesis, HAW Hamburg, 2025; 41 MTV VMA Best New Artist
  videos, 1984–2024; [PDF](https://reposit.haw-hamburg.de/bitstream/20.500.12738/19403/1/BA_Editing_shaped_the_video_star.pdf);
  translated from German) [S, small sample, not peer-reviewed]:
  - "Der Chorus weist bis in die 2010er-Jahre fast durchgehend die höchsten SPM-Werte auf": until
    the 2010s, the chorus almost always has the highest cuts per minute.
  - Pre-choruses are "überwiegend beschleunigt" (mostly faster than the section before) from 1984
    into the 2010s: "Dieses Muster verdeutlicht ihre Rolle als Spannungsaufbau vor dem Refrain"
    (their role as the tension build before the chorus).

**Counter-evidence: the wide is not the default at full volume. [N]**

- Boran Zaza, "Lessons learned: Live Multi-Camera Directing in Classical Music – Part II",
  Orchestras Canada, 2022 ([link](https://oc.ca/en/lessons-learned-live-multi-camera-directing-in-classical-music-part-ii/)):

  > "Don't default to a wide shot when there's a lot going on in the orchestra"

  He also admits that his script had too many angles "especially toward the end of the piece where
  the tension was rising". He had assumed rising tension should mean more angles, and the live show
  could not keep up with the cutting.

**Verse, intro and outro. [S] and [D]**

- Pohl 2025 (translated): "Mit 84,6 % verlangsamen sich Outros besonders häufig": outros slow down
  (against the section before) in 84.6 % of cases. The outro "fungiert damit als visuelle Beruhigung"
  (works as a visual calming). In the 1980s and early 1990s, verses were cut slower than the section
  before them, "klar als Ruhepol" (clearly the point of rest). Since the 2010s they are often the
  fastest sections, so the old verse-slow, chorus-fast pattern is an era convention, not a law.
- Cutting, "The evolution of pace in popular movies", _Cognitive Research_ 2016 (180 films;
  [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5256470/)) [S, feature films, not songs]:

  > "increasing shot durations and decreasing motion in the setup … decreasing shot durations and
  > increasing motion during the first part of the climax followed by increasing shot durations and
  > decreasing motion at the end of the climax"

  and

  > "the decreasing pace and eye movement demands of the epilogue should help bring the viewer back
  > out."

**Solos and instrumentals: go to the player. [O]**

- WorshipHouse Media guide ([link](https://worshiphousemedia.com/blogs/church-media-blog/dos-donts-live-recording)):
  "When instrumentals begin, show some band shots."

**Held high notes, drops and sing-alongs.** No source opened in this session gives a specific rule
for "the downbeat of a chorus", "a big drop" or "a held high note" in rock or pop TV. The nearest
sources are these:

- **Demme on holding (§4):** hold the great moment.
- **Brian Large** (opera and classical TV; OperaWire Q&A, [link](https://operawire.com/?p=47962)):
  > "I time everything down to the notes, and I time the phrases." … "I can say, 'Between these 16
  > bars, I want to zoom.' You stopwatch your zoom time to those 16 bars." … "It is dictated by the
  > music, by key change, by orchestration, by phrasing"
- **Zettl:** use a dissolve to change cameras "in the middle of musical phrases when it would be
  awkward to cut."

For crowd sing-alongs, see §4: crowd shots go where the crowd is visibly the event.

## 3. Movement: zoom in, zoom out, cranes and the "release" (question 2)

**A zoom-in intensifies. [D]** Zettl:

> "Zoom A fairly fast zoom-in on an event detail can be even more compelling than a simple cut to a
> closeup. In a zoom-in, the event is literally pushed into the viewer's lap. Be careful with such
> intensification devices: when overdone, the sensitive viewer will lose interest, simply as a
> defense against perceptual overkill."

**A pull-back or zoom-out opens up, distances or ends. [O], with several glossaries in agreement**

- How-To Film School, "pull back" ([link](https://howtofilmschool.com/dictionary/pull-back/)):
  - "A pull back often creates emotional distance."
  - "Directors use pull backs when they want the scene to **open up**."
  - "This is one reason the move is so common in endings, transitions, and dramatic reveals."
  - "One of the most common uses of a pull back is the **reveal**."
- Zaza (classical TV, [N]): "Don't stay too long on a close-up without starting to zoom out!"

**Is "pull back after the peak" a named practice? No.** No director's account, textbook passage or
study opened here names a "release" move that widens just after a musical peak. The parts are
documented:

1. **A wide lowers the impact** (Zettl, above). On the peak itself, a wide works against
   intensification. Just after it, a wide is the textbook way to relax the image.
2. **Pace and shot length relax after a climax:** films (Cutting 2016) and outros (Pohl 2025).
3. **The audience pay-off at the end works as a release.** A UW–Madison Cinematheque essay on
   _Stop Making Sense_ ([link](https://cinema.wisc.edu/2017/09/21/must-be-place-jonathan-demmes-stop-making-sense/))
   [O] reads it that way. Of the final number, where Demme cuts to the audience "a total of seven
   times", it says: "A release occurs as the tight grip the band held on the camera dissipates."
4. **Pull-backs are a standard way to end and to "open up"** (the glossaries above).

So Dean's refined version fits the documented logic: close and fast on the hit, then wide or opening
out as it recedes. His original wording ("at big moments … wide shots zooming out") would run
against the textbook if it means landing the wide on the peak itself. His Under Pressure cut does
not do that: every onset is on a close shot.

**Cranes, reveals and scale shots in stadium films. [O] and [N]**

- On _Eras_, Tim Grierson in Screen Daily ([link](https://www.screendaily.com/reviews/taylor-swift-the-eras-tour-review/5186838.article))
  describes Wrench "pulling back so that we can take in the thrilling lighting scheme and Mandy
  Moore's slinky choreography" and praises the film "especially when Wrench shows Swift as a rather
  small speck in a sea of fans".
- The AFI catalogue on _The Last Waltz_ ([link](https://catalog.afi.com/Catalog/moviedetails/56602))
  notes "a tower that had been erected for the sole purpose of capturing wide- angle shots". On-stage
  units had tracking moves "limited to approximately six inches".

No source opened here ties a crane pull-back to a particular song section.

## 4. Cutting: pace, the beat, holding, and crowd cutaways (question 3)

**Pace against intensity**

- [S] Lang, Zhou, Schwartz, Bolls & Potter (2000), "The effects of edits on arousal, attention, and
  memory for television messages", _J. Broadcasting & Electronic Media_ 44(1) 94–109
  ([abstract](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/the-effects-of-edits-on-arousal-attention-and-memory-for-televisi/)).
  As the edit rate rose from slow to very fast, physiological arousal, self-reported arousal and
  memory all increased: "edits can increase attention to and encoding of television message content".
  The material was TV messages, not music.
- [S] Pohl 2025: "Ein direkter Zusammenhang zwischen Musik- und Schnitttempo konnte nicht
  festgestellt werden" (no direct link between music tempo and cutting pace).
- [O] Southpoint Films ([link](https://www.southpointfilms.com/resources/how-to-direct-cameras-for-big-music-festivals)):
  "A fast-paced song requires quicker cuts, while a slow ballad should be handled with more grace."
  Also: "your cuts should still match the energy of the artist."
- [D] Zettl on the limit with few cameras:
  > "Fast cutting that reflects the fast pace of the event is also a powerful intensifier.
  > Unfortunately, it is very difficult to do in switching. Unless you have twenty cameras in the
  > field, your cutting will have to slow down after you have cut among the three or four available
  > cameras, unless you are content to return to the same shots."

**On the beat or off it: contested**

- [D] Zettl: "In music, try to cut with the beat. Cuts are like visualized bars; they determine the
  beat of the visual sequence and keep the action rhythmically tight. Cutting against the beat in
  music generally does not produce increased tension through syncopation; rather it appears as
  sloppy switching. If the general rhythm of the music is casual or flowing, use dissolves instead
  of cuts."
- [O] Two guides say the same: PremiumBeat ([link](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/multi-camera-direction-tips-live-concert/)),
  "Try as best you can to cut your cameras to the beat of the music"; WorshipHouse, "Your cuts
  should be on the beat of the music when possible."
- [N] Courtès: "Cuts have to follow the music's rhythm, even though they might not always co-occur
  with beats."
- [N] Pozzi: "Off-beat cuts are used to create dynamics: to surprise the viewer, and illustrate the
  music's emotional climax." In the Adele "Rolling in the Deep" example the paper measures, from
  02:20 the cuts "are systematically placed just before the downbeat".
- [S] Prétet & Richard 2021, 548 official music videos:
  - The commonest shot length matches one bar in "one fifth of the clips" and one beat in "two
    thirds".
  - Cuts landing on downbeats is a separate measure. "For the Performance video genre (the band is
    playing in front of the camera), we don't observe such a large correspondence (S(lbar) = 0.16)."
    That is lower than Dance (0.24) and the other main genres.
  - By music genre, the co-occurrence values are "very small … for Dance/Electronic, Hip-Hop and
    Rock".
- [O] Creative COW forum, poster Scott Sheriff ([link](https://creativecow.net/forums/thread/live-music-directing-shooting/)):
  "Cutting on the beat gets old fast." "Be unpredictable, but not distracting." "When in doubt, cut
  to a shot a little early, instead of a little late."

**Holding a shot and letting a moment breathe**

- [N] Demme, quoted by Keyframe (Fandor), original source not named: "There is great power available
  by holding on to any extended terrific moment" … "Too much cutting usually speaks to a lack of
  editorial confidence in the players and the music."
- [O] The Red Bull Music Academy essay on _Stop Making Sense_ ([link](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2014/06/stop-making-sense-feature/)):
  "Demme just holds his camera on Byrne for minutes on end, without cutting away". It also says
  "Demme had only a few planned shots per song". The UW–Madison essay cites "the four-and-a-half-minute
  take" in "Once in a Lifetime".
- [O] Screen Daily on _Eras_ praises the film for "resisting the urge to employ gimmicky quick cuts
  to artificially goose the excitement."
- [S] Cutting & Armstrong 2016, "Facial expression, size, and clutter", _Attention, Perception &
  Psychophysics_ ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4819543/)):

  > "a drop from approximately 8 to 2.5 s from extreme long shots to extreme close-ups" (24 films,
  > 1940–2010); "mean shot durations fall from about 4.5 s to approximately 2.5 s as scales increase
  > from extreme long shots to extreme close ups" (films from 2000 and 2010); "contemporary movies
  > lengthen shots that show smaller faces, and even more so with increased clutter."

  A wide full of small faces, such as Static Desk, needs the longest hold.

**Crowd cutaways**

- [N] Demme, LA Times, as quoted by UW–Madison Cinematheque: "When we were editing, we had so much
  great footage, why cut to the audience?" Cutaways "remind the movie viewers that they're watching
  a filmed record of a concert." Wikipedia, citing a 2007 Demme interview: "The only direct audience
  shots in the film occur at the very end". RBMA adds a practical reason: on the one night they
  filmed the crowd, "the combination of the bright lights and the cameras in the audience caused the
  spectators to freeze up".
- [N] Scorsese, _The Last Waltz_, in Music Connection ([link](https://www.musicconnection.com/Kubernik-the-band-the-last-waltz/)):
  "I worked on Woodstock, and that movie was about the audience. This movie is about the music."
  The AFI catalogue says "a decision was made to de-emphasize the concert audience". TCM's Jeff
  Stafford says the film avoided "gratuitous audience shots".
- [N] Paul Dugdale, RTS "Working lives" ([link](https://rts.org.uk/article/working-lives-live-event-director)):
  > "An audience makes my job easier, because they're a great barometer of the emotion in the room –
  > you can instantly show how exciting it is to be at an event by filming someone in the front row
  > going bananas."
- [O] WorshipHouse: "If there is an extended period of applause then wide crowd shots and audience
  member shots should be used."
- [O] Creative COW, poster Bill Davis: "Watch great concert film and notice that they make the crowd
  a part of the experience."

**Two camps.** Demme and Scorsese ration the crowd. Broadcast and festival coverage uses it as the
"barometer". Both agree it should be motivated: a visible reaction, applause, the end.

## 6. Empirical and academic work (question 5)

- **The field is thin.** The Southampton "Screening Live Performance" call for papers (2025;
  [link](https://screeningliveperformance.soton.ac.uk/?p=114)) says: "multi-camera production of
  live performance has generated surprisingly little research. Until quite recently, much of the
  published material appeared in trade journals, technical manuals and monographs targeted at
  professionals in the television industry."
- **No study found tests shot size against musical intensity in concert video.** What exists is
  nearby:
  - **Edit rate:** Lang et al. 2000. More edits gave more arousal and better memory.
  - **Shot scale and duration:** Cutting & Armstrong 2016. Wides are held longer, cluttered wides
    longer still.
  - **Pace across a story arc:** Cutting 2016. Shots shorten into a climax and lengthen after it.
  - **Close-ups and response:** Rooney & Bálint 2018, _Frontiers in Psychology_
    ([link](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.02349/full)).
    Close-ups raised viewers' attention to a character's mental state "when the facial expression
    was sad but not when it was neutral". That review cites Canini et al. 2011 for shot scale
    affecting self-reported arousal (not opened). So a close-up's effect depends on what the face is
    doing. A singer mid-belt is the "expressive face" case.
  - **Music-video cut timing against beat, bar and section:** Prétet & Richard 2021 (§4); Pohl 2025
    by section (§2).
- **Not opened:** Lang et al. 1999 on pacing and arousing content (abstract only, via a secondary
  summary); Benini et al. (IEEE TAC) on shot scale and arousal (403); Vernallis, _Experiencing Music
  Video_ (book). Caston's 2017 focus group with UK music-video editors
  ([PDF](https://repository.uwl.ac.uk/4706/1/EditingDossiermsmi.2017.6.pdf)) was opened and gives
  colour, not rules: "The music is your script, it's what you cut to, it's your map. The feeling you
  get, the changes in the music. The verses and the choruses…"

## 7. What this means for an auto-cut driven by the arc map

The arc map labels are peak, top, build, drop (impact, build-launch, valley), exposed note or vocal,
crowd peak and end lift. The camera classes are close (Photo Pit, Desk Tracking, Drum), wide (Static
Desk) and Stage Right (framing unknown). Each rule carries its evidence, and Dean's style is noted
where it bears.

| Arc event                                                                                                 | Rule                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | Evidence                                                                                                                                       | Dean                                                                                                                             |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Peak onset** (chorus downbeat, re-entry)                                                                | Hard cut on the downbeat, or 1–2 frames before it, to a close of whoever leads (Pit on the singer).                                                                                                                                          | Zettl (tight intensifies; cut with the beat); Pozzi (just before the downbeat)                                                                 | **Agrees.** Every UP peak onset is on a Photo Pit close.                                                                         |
| **Inside a peak**                                                                                         | Faster: aim for 2.5–3.5 s mean shot length (Dean's UP peaks: 2.8 cuts per 10 s, about 3.6 s). Do not reuse the angle just used (only 4–5 cameras). Snap cuts to beats, but not every bar, and vary the bar position.                         | Zettl; Lang; Pohl; Prétet & Richard (performance videos rarely lock to downbeats); Sheriff (forum)                                             | **Agrees** (2.8 against 2.0 cuts per 10 s; 62 against 50 % close).                                                               |
| **Release** (intensity falling from a peak but above the baseline; the 0.5–3 s decay after an impact hit) | Open out: cut to Static Desk, or zoom out slowly on it, and hold for at least 4 s (wides are read slowly, and a crowded wide more slowly). Time the zoom to the phrase or bars (Large), not to a fixed duration.                             | Zettl (wider means less impact); Cutting 2016 (shots lengthen after the climax); Cutting & Armstrong (hold wides longer); pull-back glossaries | **Agrees with the refined version.** UP opens wide with a dynamic zoom 0.5–2.1 s after 3 of 4 peak onsets. Not a named practice. |
| **Build** (pre-chorus, climb, fills)                                                                      | Speed the cutting up and/or **push in** (zoom in) towards the peak. Save the wide for after it.                                                                                                                                              | Pohl (pre-choruses mostly faster, "Spannungsaufbau"); Pozzi ("accelerate near climaxes"); Zettl (zoom-in intensifies)                          | Untested. ARC-FOR-VIDEO's earlier "wide shots in builds" came from one unverified guide; this evidence points the other way.     |

**Where Dean's style differs from or goes beyond the literature**

- **"Wide, zooming out, at big moments" (the original wording) differs** from the textbook mapping,
  where wide means less impact, if it means on the moment. His own cut does not do that: the onsets
  are close.
- **His refined version ("as it recedes, still in the moment") is consistent with** documented
  practice at the song scale: release after the climax, longer shots, and wides that ease the
  pressure. It is **more specific than anything found**: a wide 0.5–2 s after the onset is his own
  move, not a named convention. Treat it as Dean's house style, with documented logic behind it.
- **Timing check for the auto-cut:** in UP the wide arrives 0.5–2.1 s after the _onset_, which is
  still inside the peak section. That matches "recede" only if the peak is a hit whose energy decays
  within a second or two. If it is a sustained chorus, it does not. The arc map should place the
  release in the decay after the hit (impact-type peaks), or at the section-level fall (chorus to
  post-chorus). It should not be placed at a fixed offset from every onset.
- **Still to confirm:** the direction of the zoom on UP V1 shots 20, 35 and 52. If they are
  zoom-ins, the pattern becomes a push-in on the wide, which fits Zettl's zoom-in as intensifier,
  and the release reading falls away.
